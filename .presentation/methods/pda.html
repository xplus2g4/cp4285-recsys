PD and PDA

Causal Intervention for Leveraging Popularity Bias in Recommendation — Zhang et al., SIGIR 2021

Popularity bias Causal adjustment of the score Training loss Inference score MF in the paper · model-agnostic score

PD and PDA treat item popularity as a confounder: it affects both which videos the platform showed and how likely users were to click them. Both methods train the backbone with an extra popularity term, so the backbone's score f(u, i) no longer has to explain popularity. PD (Popularity-bias Deconfounding) ranks by f alone and so removes popularity from the ranking. PDA (… and Adjusting) adds back the popularity of the most recent training period, which the paper treats as a forecast of what will stay popular.

Headline result. PD lowers ARP@10 from to at γ = 0.2, and pays for it with GAUC on clicks. PDA (γ ≥ 0.05) gains accuracy under every label (GAUC click about +0.012), but every user gets nearly the same list: coverage@10 drops to about 0.004 (about 28–38 of 7,344 items), and the mean video length in the lists drops from s to 38–55 s. This is our clearest evidence that popularity and duration are coupled on KuaiRand.

The problem it targets

The target is popularity bias. In the standard log, the 20% most-exposed items (the head) receive 76% of all training exposures. A model trained on these rows sees popular videos clicked far more often.

The paper's causal reading has three parts:

  • Popularity → exposure. The platform's own recommender showed popular items more often.
  • Popularity → click. Users click popular items partly because they are popular (the conformity effect). The paper keeps this path: it is part of real user behaviour.
  • The problem. Popularity is a common cause of both. The observed click rate of an item therefore mixes "this user likes it" with "this item was pushed and is popular". A standard model such as Naive MF cannot separate the two and learns to rank popular items high for everyone. The paper calls this bias amplification.

KuaiRand example. Naive MF's top-10 lists are % long-tail items (APLT@10 = ). Their items average training exposures each. The pool average is about 152 (1,117,036 train rows ÷ 7,344 items).

The paper's twist. Not all popularity is bad. Some items are popular because they are good or because they are trending now. So the paper first removes popularity (PD) and then adds back a chosen, "desired" popularity at inference (PDA).

How it works

Symbols

f(u, i)
The user-item matching score from the backbone: the MF logit (user bias + item bias + embedding dot product).
stage t
The training window is cut into 10 equal-time stages. Each training row belongs to the stage in which it was logged.
mit
The local popularity of item i in stage t: its share of that stage's exposures, scaled so that an item with an average share has m = 1.
m̃i
The popularity used at inference: the item's local popularity in the last training stage.
γ
The training exponent. It sets how much of the click signal the model may attribute to popularity. This is the knob.
γ̃
The inference exponent. It sets how much popularity is added back at ranking time. PD uses γ̃ = 0; PDA uses γ̃ = γ, as the paper does.

Training (same for PD and PDA)

  1. Cut the training window into 10 equal-time stages. For every item and stage, count exposures and compute mit:
    mit = (cit + 1) ÷ (Nt + nitems) × nitems
    Here cit is the item's exposure count in stage t, Nt is the stage's total exposure count, and nitems = 7,344. The "+1" keeps items with no exposures in a stage finite.
  2. For each logged exposure of item i to user u in stage t, predict the click logit as interest plus popularity:
    logit(u, i, t) = f(u, i) + γ · log mit
  3. Train with BCE on the click label, exactly as Naive MF. The popularity term has no parameters, so only f learns. Because the popularity term already explains the popularity part of the click rate, f is left with the rest. The paper calls this deconfounding.

Inference

  1. PD: rank items by f(u, i) only.
    scorePD(u, i) = f(u, i)
    The paper justifies this with backdoor adjustment: average the click probability over all popularity values instead of using the item's own popularity, P(c | do(u, i)) = Σz P(c | u, i, z) P(z). "do(u, i)" means "as if the item had been shown regardless of its popularity". That average increases with f, so ranking by f is exact.
  2. PDA: add back last-stage popularity.
    scorePDA(u, i) = f(u, i) + γ̃ · log m̃i
    The extra term is the same for every user. It lifts items that were popular recently, not items that were popular over the whole window.

Our form versus the paper's

The paper writes the click probability as a product, P(c = 1 | u, i, m) = ELU′(f) · mγ, and trains with BPR (a pairwise loss that ranks a clicked item above a sampled unclicked one). ELU′ is a function that keeps the interest score positive (ef for f ≤ 0, else f + 1). Our pipeline trains every method with pointwise BCE on all logged exposures. So we apply the same product to the odds (probability ÷ (1 − probability)) instead:

odds(c = 1) = ef(u, i) · (mit)γ  ⇔  logit = f(u, i) + γ · log mit

The idea is unchanged: interest and popularity are separate factors, and popularity enters with the exponent γ. Only the scale the factors multiply on changes. PD's ranking by f stays exact, because Σz P(z) σ(f + γ log z) increases with f.

What the knob does

  • γ = 0 (and γ̃ = 0): identical to Naive MF. We checked this: same calibration GAUC (0.54643) at the same best epoch (18).
  • Larger γ: training attributes more of each item's click rate to popularity. PD then removes more popularity from the ranking. PDA adds back more of the last-stage popularity, because γ̃ = γ.
  • The paper searched γ in [0.02, 0.25]; we sweep six values in that range.

Recommender family

  1. Paper. The paper uses one family: a latent factor model, MF trained with BPR ("we choose the simple Matrix Factorization (MF) in this work"). It also applies the inference adjustment (but not the deconfounded training) to two other scorers, BPRMF-A and DICE-A, by replacing f with their predictions. The paper reports no LightGCN, sequential or feature-based experiments.
  2. Model-agnostic? Yes in design. The paper separates f from popularity so that the framework is "extendable to any collaborative filtering model that concerns user-item matching". It needs: a scalar user-item score f, a timestamp on each training row (to assign stages), and item exposure (or interaction) counts per stage. It needs no side features and no extra parameters.
  3. What we used. The shared 64-dimensional MF backbone, trained with BCE on clicks: a CF model.
  4. Other families (reasoning, not evidence).
    • Graph CF (e.g. LightGCN): the additive term plugs in unchanged, since LightGCN also outputs a dot-product score. One open point: graph propagation itself spreads popularity through high-degree nodes, and a single additive term may not absorb that.
    • Sequential models: they already see recent behaviour, so some of what PDA re-injects may already be in f. Stage-level popularity is also coarse next to a session-level model.
    • LLM-based recommenders: PDA's inference step works on any per-item score or logit. PD's training step needs control of the training loss, i.e. fine-tuning with the extra term.

Our implementation

Knob grid

γ ∈ {0.02, 0.05, 0.1, 0.15, 0.2, 0.25}. Each value runs twice: once as PD (γ̃ = 0) and once as PDA (γ̃ = γ). That is 12 sweeps and 60 final runs. Every final run was evaluated on the test split exactly once.

Model selection on the calibration split scores the inference-time output: f for PD and the adjusted score for PDA. The paper's Algorithm 1 does the same.

Diagnostics

PD/PDA log no method-specific diagnostics (no weights, no propensities). Checks run for the PR:

  • γ = γ̃ = 0 reproduces Naive MF exactly (calibration GAUC 0.54643, best epoch 18 at lr 1e-2, wd 1e-5).
  • Reloading the saved model and rescoring the calibration candidates matches the saved scores (max |Δ| = 0).
  • Unit tests check that each row gets its own stage's popularity, that the latest row falls in the last stage, and that items with no exposures stay finite.

Discussion

Why PD costs accuracy here. PD's training term γ log mt absorbs the click-rate advantage that goes with popularity. Ranking by f alone then demotes items whose clicks were explained by popularity. On the random-exposure test, those items are still clicked more, so accuracy drops. Likely because much of KuaiRand's popularity is the "benign" kind the paper itself describes (quality, conformity), which also holds under random exposure. Two side effects follow (interpretation). First, coverage falls: the lists move to a small set of mid-popularity items whose f is high for everyone. Second, clickbait share rises: clickbait items have high click rates for their popularity, and "clicks beyond popularity" is exactly what f keeps.

Why PDA gains accuracy but collapses the lists. The term γ̃ log m̃i is the same for every user. From γ ≈ 0.05 it outweighs most user-specific differences in f, so nearly every user gets the same ~30 recently popular videos (see the demo). These videos are clicked and finished more often even under random exposure, which gives the accuracy gain. Likely because the random log starts on 22 April, right after the last training stage, so last-stage popularity is a good short-term forecast. The watch-ratio gain (about +0.04) is three times the click gain (about +0.012). That fits short videos, which are easier to watch to the end.

Research questions. RQ1: PD reduces its own bias; PDA increases it, by design. RQ2: PD raises clickbait share and lowers coverage; PDA lowers clickbait share and narrows the activity gap but destroys coverage. RQ3: PDA is the clearest evidence of bias coupling in the panel. Re-injecting popularity cuts mean list duration by 23–41 s, because the recently popular videos it lifts are short. In the training data, the 200 most-exposed items have a median duration of 67 s, against 81 s for the pool. Across the whole pool, the rank correlation between recent popularity and duration is weak (about −0.04, from the data facts), so the coupling sits in the head rather than across all items. This is the reverse direction of H1: a popularity correction moves duration. H3: not supported for PD at γ ≤ 0.2. Mainstream and non-mainstream users lose about equally, and the gap does not widen. Under PDA (γ ≥ 0.05), non-mainstream users gain about +0.012 to +0.015 NDCG@10, while mainstream users change less (−0.004 to +0.005). RQ4: in the ARP–GAUC plane, PD traces a frontier that trades accuracy for lower popularity. PDA at γ = 0.02 is slightly better than Naive MF on both ARP and GAUC click, but the effects are tiny (ARP −4, GAUC +0.0009). PDA at γ ≥ 0.05 is the most accurate click-trained setting on this page, at the cost of the least diverse lists.

Open questions. (1) How much of PDA's accuracy step survives if the lr is fixed at Naive MF's 0.01? (2) Would a version with both popularity and duration as confounders keep PD's popularity reduction without the clickbait rise, or keep PDA's accuracy without the short-video collapse?

Caveats

  • The bootstrap CIs of set metrics (coverage, entropy, Gini) are biased.
  • There is no multiple-comparison correction across the panel.
  • * covers user sampling only, not seed variance. Read it together with the seed std.
  • Satisfaction cells rest on about 10% of test users.
  • Optimiser confound. PD at γ = 0.25 and PDA at γ ≥ 0.05 use a different tuned lr / wd than Naive MF. Their Δ mixes the method with the optimisation change.
  • Sparse forecast. m̃ comes from the last stage only (27,619 rows, about 3.8 exposures per item). The paper's trend forecast (b) was not tried.
  • Different test regime from the paper. The paper tests on the next time stage, whose popularity matches the forecast. We test on random exposure. PDA's accuracy gains here likely also depend on the random log starting right after the training window.
  • ARP measures overall popularity, not last-stage popularity. It understates how concentrated PDA's lists are; APLT, coverage and RSP_pop show it better.
  • Several PDA rows have seed std ≈ 0 on list metrics (e.g. C_KL_pop, RSP_pop). All seeds recommend nearly the same items, so seed variance there says little about robustness.
