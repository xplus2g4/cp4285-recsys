What does fixing one bias do to the others?

Trade-offs of single-bias debiasing methods on KuaiRand — progress report (6 methods, 54 sweeps, 270 test-evaluated models)

How to read this page. Words with a dotted underline are defined in the glossary; hover them to see the definition. Every chart is interactive: hover a point for its numbers. Each method has its own page with an interactive explanation of how it works.

1. Goal and motivation

Recommender systems learn from logs that the previous recommender produced. Those logs carry several biases at the same time: users only react to what they were shown (exposure bias), popular items dominate (popularity bias), and for video, watch-time targets depend on video length (duration bias).

Most debiasing methods are built and evaluated for one of these biases. The main survey of the field states this directly:

“From former studies, we can find that existing methods are usually designed for just addressing one or two specific biases. However, in the real world, various biases usually occur simultaneously. … It is imperative that recommender systems require a general debiasing framework to handle the mixture of biases. … Although challenging, the simple case — the mixture of just two or three biases — is worth to be explored first.” J. Chen, H. Dong, X. Wang, F. Feng, M. Wang, X. He. Bias and Debias in Recommender System: A Survey and Future Directions. ACM TOIS 41(3), 2023, §5.2 (arXiv:2010.03240). §4.5 makes the same point: “Most existing methods are designed for addressing one or two biases of a specific scenario.”

The same group repeats the observation as the motivation for AutoDebias (Chen et al., SIGIR 2021): existing methods “target one or two specific biases” and need hand-set configurations.

Research goal. Before building a method that handles several biases, we need to know what the single-bias methods already do to the biases they do not target. We measure these off-target effects for each method, trace how they change with the method's main hyperparameter, and try to explain them from the method's mechanism.

Research questions

  • RQ1 — Own bias. Measured on randomly exposed data, how much does each method reduce the bias it targets?
  • RQ2 — Other biases. How does each method change the biases it does not target?
  • RQ3 — Coupling. Are duration and popularity linked in KuaiRand, so that fixing one moves the other?
  • RQ4 — Frontier. Which methods and settings lie on the Pareto frontier of accuracy against the bias metrics, and which are dominated?

Hypotheses (stated before the experiments)

  • H1. Duration correction (D2Q) moves recommendations toward short videos, and through the coupling also shifts popularity.
  • H2. Exposure correction (IPS) raises long-tail coverage, but also raises variance across seeds and lowers precision on tail items.
  • H3. Popularity debiasing narrows the gap between items but widens the gap between users, with mainstream users losing accuracy.

Bias panel (what we measure)

Every model is scored on the same panel. Accuracy is measured under all five labels, whatever the training label, so a method cannot “win” just by changing the target.

Protocol (identical for every method)

  • Each method has one knob, and each knob value is one sweep: tune lr × weight decay (9 points) on calibration, then train 5 seeds and evaluate each on test exactly once. Every knob value is reported, so each method gives a curve, not a single point.
  • Δ is reported against Naive MF (click-trained methods) or VR (D2Q). The * marker means the paired bootstrap CI excludes 0. Read it next to the seed std, because the CI ignores seed variance.
  • Caveats that apply everywhere: bootstrap CIs of set metrics (coverage, entropy, Gini) are biased; there is no multiple-comparison correction; satisfaction cells rest on about 10% of test users.

4. Methods studied

All six methods are implemented on the same MF backbone so that only the correction differs. They cover three biases and four mechanisms. All six are model-agnostic in design: they need a user-item score, plus item popularity (PD/PDA, IPS-pop), durations (D2Q), side features (IPS-feat) or user and item embeddings (MACR). In their papers, only MACR was tested on a graph model (LightGCN). None was tested on a sequential or LLM-based recommender. Each method page discusses what would carry over.

5.3 Answers so far

RQ1 — Own bias: mostly yes

  • IPS-pop / SNIPS give the largest popularity reduction: ARP 1,998 → 509 (clip 10) → 133 (no clip), with a small GAUC gain (+0.0012 to +0.0018).
  • PD lowers ARP (to 559 at γ = 0.2), but GAUC click falls with it (by up to 0.023).
  • MACR lowers ARP only moderately (to 880 at c = 100). Likely cause: its learned item term σ(ŷ_i) is almost constant (0.98–1.00 in a calibration check on one model), so subtracting it barely reorders items.
  • D2Q removes VR's long-video preference: 184.5 s → 66–78 s.
  • PDA raises popularity on purpose, because it re-injects recent popularity. It is the most accurate method on every label.

RQ2 — Other biases: every method has off-target effects

  • IPS-pop: duration fairness gets worse (RSP_dur, C_KL_dur up), and clickbait share rises from 0.066 to 0.09–0.11.
  • PDA: lists become much shorter (78.5 → 38–55 s) and collapse onto about 30 items (coverage 0.004). Clickbait share drops to about 0.
  • D2Q: popularity falls (ARP 2,319 → 513–848), but coverage collapses and NDCG on raw watch time drops by about 0.02.
  • IPS-feat: lists get longer (+23.5 s at clip 4000) and closer to users' duration history (C_KL_dur down). It has less clickbait.
  • MACR c ≥ 10 and PD: coverage falls and clickbait rises.

RQ3 — Coupling: yes, but only at the head, and it depends on the mechanism

  • Moving into the head shortens lists. PDA's lists are the most popular and the shortest. This matches the data: the top 200 items have a median of 67 s against 81 s for the pool.
  • Moving into the tail does not lengthen lists by itself. IPS-pop cuts ARP by up to 93%, but changes the mean duration by at most 7.5 s.
  • IPS-feat does lengthen lists. Its propensity uses duration deciles among its features. So the duration shift comes from what the correction conditions on, not from popularity alone.

RQ4 — Frontier (GAUC click × ARP, click-trained methods)

  • Non-dominated: PDA γ = 0.1–0.2 (most accurate), IPS-pop / SNIPS clip 30–∞ (least popular), and IPS-feat with no clip (in between).
  • Dominated: PD at every γ and MACR at every c. On NDCG × ARP, MACR c = 10–20 and IPS-feat clip 300 reach the frontier, because they lose almost no NDCG.
  • Naive MF is dominated on every pair of axes we checked.

H1 (duration → short, shifts popularity): supported

D2Q cuts mean list duration by 106–118 s against VR and lowers ARP by 1,471–1,806. The quantile ranking (D2Q-Q) goes further, to 30–43 s.

H2 (IPS: tail coverage ↑, variance ↑): partly

Coverage rises only at clip ≥ 100; at clip 10–30 it falls. IPS-pop's seed std is not inflated. IPS-feat's ARP seed std is large (185–367 at clip 300–2000). Tail precision has not been measured directly yet.

H3 (user gap widens, mainstream users lose): not supported so far

Under PD and IPS-pop, NDCG falls about equally for mainstream and non-mainstream users. Their gap changes by at most 0.003 and is not significant, except at PD γ = 0.25, where it narrows by 0.014. PDA (γ ≥ 0.05) narrows the activity gap by 0.010–0.024.

6. Status

Done

  • A frozen, guarded split with a hashed manifest, and a soft guard so the test split is used once.
  • A shared MF backbone, training loop, sweep runner and bias panel with a user-level bootstrap.
  • Six methods plus the VR baseline: 54 sweeps, 270 test-evaluated models. Each method is in its own reviewed PR (#1–#6).

Not done (from the methodology)

  • Doubly robust (DR), AutoDebias (a joint, multi-bias reference) and DICE.
  • A second backbone (DeepFM or LightGCN). The side features are already preprocessed.
  • Training on the other labels (watch ratio, long view) for every method: the full label matrix.
  • The 5 × 5 popularity × duration grid, and mass-shift heatmaps showing where each method moves recommendations.
  • Paired tests with Holm correction, the sensitivity run on the full split, and feedback-loop simulation.

7. Questions for discussion

The audit already shows clear trade-offs. The main decision is which direction to take next. Possible directions:

A. Explain the coupling (depth)

Build the 5 × 5 popularity × duration grid and the mass-shift heatmaps. Then decompose each method's list shift into a popularity part and a duration part. This turns RQ3 from an observation into an explanation, e.g. “a correction moves duration only if its propensity or target depends on duration.”

B. Test multi-bias methods (breadth)

Add DR and AutoDebias, and possibly DICE. Ask whether methods designed for several biases avoid the off-target effects we found. This directly answers the survey's open problem.

C. Check robustness

Repeat the audit with a second backbone (DeepFM or LightGCN) and with other training labels. This shows whether the trade-offs belong to the method or to the MF + click setup.

D. Propose a correction

Use the findings to design a joint popularity + duration correction, e.g. an IPS-style propensity that conditions on both. Then show that it moves the frontier. This makes the project a method paper.

E. Wrap up now

Write up the six-method audit as it is: audit matrix, frontiers and RQ verdicts, with limitations. Run the paired tests with Holm correction first.

Specific questions

Each method page also ends with one or two method-specific open questions in its Discussion section.

  1. Contribution. Is a careful measurement study (an audit of off-target effects) a sufficient contribution, or does the project need a new method (direction D)?
    This decides between A/B/C and D.
  2. Depth or breadth. If audit only: is it better to explain one coupling well (A) or to cover more methods and backbones (B/C)?
    Our best finding (RQ3: the coupling depends on what the correction conditions on) would benefit most from A.
  3. Is PDA “debiasing”? PDA raises accuracy everywhere by using recent popularity, and makes lists more popular and shorter. Should it be framed as a debiasing method, or as a separate class (“bias exploitation”) in the analysis?
    This changes how RQ1 is scored for PDA and MACR (c = 0).
  4. Tuning protocol. We select lr / weight decay by calibration GAUC (NDCG for raw watch time). For some knob values the tuned learning rate changes (PD γ = 0.25, PDA, MACR c = 0, IPS clip ≥ 100), which mixes optimisation effects into the curve. Should we fix the backbone hyperparameters across knob values instead?
  5. What counts as “better”. Several panel metrics have no single good direction (mean duration, C_KL_pop overshooting below the user's history). Should the frontier analysis use distance-to-reference metrics only (W1, C_KL), or report them separately?
  6. Statistics. Are 5 seeds plus a paired user bootstrap acceptable, or should seed variance enter the test, e.g. a hierarchical bootstrap over seeds and users? Example: SNIPS's apparent halving of the ARP seed std at clip 1000 comes from a single seed and is not significant by an F-test. Holm correction across the panel × 54 sweeps will also remove many small effects.
  7. Evaluation pool. Top-10 lists are ranked over all 7,344 items, but relevance only exists for the randomly exposed candidates. Is this mixed protocol acceptable, or should list metrics also be computed on candidate sets only?
  8. Scope and timeline. What is the target: a course report, a workshop paper or a conference paper? This sets how much of the “Not done” list is needed.
